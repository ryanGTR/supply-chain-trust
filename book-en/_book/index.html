Preface: Why a Bank IT Engineer Wrote a Book About Trust

I work in IT at a financial institution. Banking life runs on governance, audit, and change management — we have an entire process for how a single piece of code reaches production. Then one day an uncomfortable thought landed: that process governs the few thousand lines we wrote ourselves, while the system actually running in production is hundreds of thousands of lines of dependencies written by other people. Where did that code come from? Has anyone tampered with it? On what grounds do we trust it? I could not answer.

Rather than write yet another supply-chain security checklist, I decided to do something slow and thorough: start at the bottom, with the cryptographic primitives, and build “trust” back up one layer at a time by hand — what a signature actually guarantees, why a certificate deserves belief, how encryption and signing break when you combine them in the obvious order — and only then map those primitives onto the software supply chain, so that underneath fashionable words like SBOM, artifact signing, and provenance you can see exactly which blocks are carrying the load.

This book is the record of that trip.

Who this book is for

NoteSet your expectations before you start

This book is written for:

  • Backend, platform, and DevOps engineers who run npm install, mvn package, and docker pull every day but have never worked out — or have avoided working out — why any step in that chain deserves trust;
  • Security, audit, and governance people who understand controls but want to know what the cryptography underneath a control actually guarantees, and what it very much does not;
  • Java engineers like me: the analogies and code lean on Java (Signature, keystores, cacerts) because that is my native language, and it may be yours.

What you need first: the ability to read a medium-sized piece of code in any language, and comfort at a command line. What you do not need: a cryptography background. The mental models get built as we go, and the maths is deliberately left out — what stays is the intuition for why it has to work this way.

The through-line

One sentence runs through the whole book. Hold on to it as you read:

Trust is not computed. It is granted. Cryptography only pins a human trust decision in place with mathematics — making it verifiable, tamper-evident, and transferable.

Part I builds the three primitives: hashes, digital signatures, and MACs. Part II maps them onto a real production line: the requirements in NIST’s SSDF, artifact signing in CI/CD, and key custody. Part III turns to what comes in — dependencies, SBOMs, and what you are actually trusting when you trust a package you did not write.

There is one more discipline, borrowed from my previous book: predict → test → explain. Before every important conclusion I will stop and ask you to guess. Wherever something can be run, you get a script you can actually run (examples/) — sign a file, flip one byte, watch verification fail in front of you. The places where your guess was wrong are the places where you learn something.

Three ways to read this

Path For whom How to walk it
🟢 Ten-minute skim Deciding whether the book is worth your time Read Chapter 1 plus the “What to take away” box at the end of each chapter
🟡 The full trip You want the mental model to actually stick Read in order, genuinely stop at every “predict first”, and run the scripts in examples/
🔵 Borrow a pattern You have a concrete problem (sign an artifact, validate a certificate chain) Jump to the capability-boundary table in Chapter 5 to locate your problem, then read back into the matching chapter

Chapter 4 (authenticated encryption) is marked deep dive, skippable. The attacks it explains are lovely, but skipping it on a first pass costs you nothing on the main line.

Honest boundaries

ImportantWhat this book is, and what it is not
  • This is a learning record and a portfolio, not a product, and not a basis for the security design of your environment. I verified the conclusions as best I could — but read them sceptically, because scepticism is exactly the habit this book is trying to teach.
  • Every scenario is described as “a financial institution” or “a typical banking environment”, and the material has been de-identified: no real organisation names, no internal systems, no keys, no configuration.
  • I am not a cryptographer. I am an engineer who writes down what he does not understand and then closes the gap step by step. For rigorous proofs, read the original papers (they are in the references).
